# Desktop WebRTC, rooms and their tests

Build notes, moved here from `AGENTS.md` as they were: what's built, what code relies on, the tests and the measurements. `AGENTS.md` has the rules every session needs and the index of these files. The headings below are that file's sections as they were, and "see <Section>" names one of them: the index says which files each is in. What a note calls "below" or "above" may be in another of these files now: search `notes/` for its name.

## Building

### Web builds

- `tide_platform_web_rooms` puts two players in a room: a relay on this machine (`relay/`), and WebRTC between two frames of one page in headless Chrome or Edge, in real time rather than the other web tests' virtual time (`platform/tests/web_rooms.mjs`); `tide_platform_web_rooms_handover` puts three there, whose host leaves, to check host migration: once the page tells it both players say they're in (a message into its frame, which the program asks about with `tide_web_eval`), never after a time; `tide_platform_web_rooms_ended` checks that a match its host ended stays ended for a player who missed the goodbye. With `TIDE_RELAY` set, like `wss://relay.tide-engine.dev`, that script checks a deployed relay instead, and with `TIDE_ICE_POLICY=relay` too, its TURN (every packet through it). The relay's own tests run wherever there's Node (`node --test relay/relay.test.mjs`).
- Desktop rooms have tests of their own in every native build:
  - `tide_platform_rtc` checks each piece of `platform/src/rtc/` (crypto against the RFCs' values), then the pieces together, two peers on this machine over real UDP. Its tests of what a connection must not do (see Networking, Desktop WebRTC) are each made so that nothing rests on how fast the machine is: an ICE agent whose check is answered, rightly signed, from another socket than the one it checked, where the test goes on once the agent's own state says it read the answer; an ICE agent that's checked from one socket before it has their password, and is then given it with another socket's address, which ranks higher, at a time that stands still, so the one check that goes in turn goes there, and the socket their check came from only gets its check back if that goes out of turn; SCTP between two of ours with one kind of packet never arriving (the channel's acknowledgement, its open, our COOKIE ACKs), in made-up time; a peer against a far side the test makes by hand of the same pieces (`far_side` in `platform/tests/rtc.c`: an ICE agent, DTLS and SCTP of its own, so the test says what it sends, when, in which datagram, and what of the peer's it never gets: its ClientHello goes by hand, from its socket, before it has read any of the peer's checks, and in another test before the peer has its answer, with time standing still from there, so no flight is ever sent again); and two peers of ours whose host, the DTLS client, is never updated again once its ICE has a pair, so the joiner only ever reads a ClientHello that the update which found the pair sent. A peer's clock is whoever updates it's (`rtc_peer_update` takes the time), so there the test stops it, or makes it up, once what's tested begins: with time standing still nothing is sent again, and what the test waits for can only come one way.
  - `tide_platform_rtc_vectors` (`platform/tests/rtc_vectors.mjs`) checks the crypto against Node's (OpenSSL's) on random inputs every run, and Node checks our signatures.
  - `tide_platform_rtc_fuzz` feeds everything that reads other machines' messages (JSON, SDP, STUN and TURN, certificates, DTLS, SCTP, WebSocket frames) real messages broken at random, with checksums and signatures made right again. Configure with `-DTIDE_SANITIZE=ON` (Linux and macOS) for AddressSanitizer and UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer, and give it more rounds: `tide_platform_rtc_fuzz 20000 <seed>`.
  - `tide_platform_rooms` (`platform/tests/rooms.mjs`) puts two desktop players in a match through a relay on this machine, and three in one whose host leaves (host migration), then each browser it finds (headless Chrome or Edge, and Firefox) in a desktop room and a desktop player in that browser's room, with the browser showing its address and hiding it. The same `TIDE_RELAY` (like `wss://relay.tide-engine.dev`) and `TIDE_ICE_POLICY` check a deployed relay and its TURN.
  - On a relay on this machine, desktop players' sockets stay on loopback (`TIDE_RTC_LOCAL`), so Windows' firewall has nothing to ask; Firefox on Windows offers no loopback candidates, so its cases use ordinary sockets. `TIDE_RTC_DEBUG=1` has the desktop side say what it does.
  - A browser's `--user-data-dir` (or Firefox's `--profile`) must be an absolute folder that exists, or the browser shows a dialog, even headless. Firefox needs `--wait-for-browser`, or stopping it leaves the browser running, and `MOZ_DISABLE_SAFE_MODE_KEY`: on Windows, Shift held as it starts (anyone typing on the machine) puts it in safe mode, whose prompt nobody answers headless, so it never opens the page.
  - Chrome is stopped by asking it to close (`Browser.close` on `--remote-debugging-pipe`): killing its processes from outside can miss one it starts again meanwhile, which keeps the browser from going. Each case starts once the last browser is gone, and its deadlines start once the page says it loaded, since a browser's first start on a fresh machine (CI) can take most of a minute.
  - What a desktop player waits for, it's told on stdin once it happened, never given a time: the handover's host leaves once both players say they're in, and a host ends its match once it says the relay has its room (`TIDE_RTC_DEBUG`).
  - A case that fails ends with what each side said, each line with when (`account` in `rooms.mjs`): the lines that showed as they came, what every desktop player's stack said of its connection meanwhile (`TIDE_RTC_DEBUG`, which the test sets for all of them and shows only then: the relay's messages, ICE's pairs, DTLS's and SCTP's handshakes, the channel's open and its acknowledgement, the first message each way, and why a peer went), and how the browser's page last said it stands, which it says once a second (its connection's, DTLS's and SCTP's states, its channel's, how many messages the channel got and echoed, and what a send refused): how old that is says whether the page still ran. The test cuts what a player prints and what its stack says (stderr) into lines apart (`take` in `player`), and the stack writes each of its lines whole (`rtc_debug`): read through one buffer, a line a case waits for could land in the middle of a line of the other stream and never be seen, which the cases whose host already ran with `TIDE_RTC_DEBUG` were open to, and which failed one of them twice in four runs once every player had it.
  - That account is there for a failure it would have explained and the log didn't (CI's Macs, 2026-10-06, once: "a desktop player joins a room Chrome hosts" ended with the browser's `open` and then nothing back in 45 seconds, with no room failed). Its cause isn't known. What the log does say: Chrome's `open` comes when our channel's open message arrives, before its own association is up or its acknowledgement goes out, so it only says our SCTP got that far; no failure on our side in 45 seconds says ICE's consent, DTLS and SCTP all held; and nothing more from the browser says our socket went on answering it (one that goes without a word has Chrome say `ice disconnected` 6 seconds later, and `state failed` after 16). Under those, three things give the very same lines, each made to happen with a fault put into the stack by hand, and each told apart by the account: Chrome's association never coming up (our COOKIE ACKs lost: it asks again after 1, 2, 4, 8 seconds and on, and its SCTP stays `connecting`), its acknowledgement of the channel never counting on our side, and our messages going nowhere. A page that stopped running would give them too, which wasn't tried: how old its last word is says so. What didn't bring it back: 150 runs on Windows, 50 more with every core busy (the case then takes up to 8 seconds, most of it making the desktop's identity), any one packet of the SCTP handshake lost once, theirs or ours, our last flight of DTLS lost once, an INIT of theirs dropped so both sides send one (three orders of their crossing were seen), and every update of the stack made to take up to 0.4 seconds.
  - Three weaknesses of the stack found while looking for it are fixed since (2026-10-06; see Networking, Desktop WebRTC), none of them shown to be its cause. The first changes what two of those three faults give: with our COOKIE ACKs lost, or the channel's acknowledgement never counting, the desktop side says `sctp: gave up connecting` 20 seconds after its association started, its peer goes, and the player's room fails (`tide: couldn't connect to the host of room`), so the case ends there and says why, not at its 45 seconds with no room failed (seen against Chrome, with the acknowledgement dropped by hand). Messages that go nowhere on a channel that's open still end at the case's own deadline.

## Networking

### Lobbies on the relay

- The relay keeps lobbies beside its rooms (built 2026-10-11, the service side of the lobby and matchmaking design; the owner asked for a service of ours that games test with, Steam and EOS being the others, with parties and matchmaking not ruled out by its shape; that it extends the relay, and everything of its API, are Claude's picks). `relay/lobbies.mjs`, which `createRelay` makes one of (`lobbyLimits`) and whose `handle` its HTTP server asks first. Not deployed: the relay on Fly is the owner's to update. What's built, and what code relies on:
  - **A lobby is a group of players, and a match is something attached to it:** `{id, game, name, owner, max, public, open, data, match, version, members}`, a member `{id, name, data}`. A party is a lobby with no `match`. `data` is text by key, the lobby's and each member's, which is all a list filters by and all a game's own state there (ready, a character) needs. `match` is the game's to write and read: with Tide's rooms, the room's code.
  - **Plain HTTP with JSON,** so a game needs requests and nothing else (`Web.Request`), from a desktop or a page: `POST /v1/lobbies`, `GET /v1/lobbies?game=`, `POST /v1/lobbies/quick`, `GET /v1/lobbies/ID?since=`, and `POST /v1/lobbies/ID/{join,leave,member,update,kick,owner}`. The file's head has each. Any site's pages may ask (`access-control-allow-origin: *`, and the preflight), and nothing is cached.
  - **A player is a secret it keeps,** sent as `Authorization: Bearer`: its ID is 16 hex digits of a SHA-256 of it, so members see each other's IDs and nobody's secret. No accounts and nothing stored: a name is what the player says. A Steam identity the service verifies isn't built.
  - **A member is there while it asks.** `GET .../ID?since=<version>` is held until the lobby changes or `pollSeconds` (20) pass, so a member that asks again each time it's answered sees changes as they happen with no socket, and one that hasn't asked for `goneSeconds` (45) is taken out by the sweep (every 5 s, only while there are lobbies). One whose answer is being held is there (`member.asking`); a request whose connection closes lets go at once. Nothing is timed but those two limits, both on the relay's clock.
  - **The relay is the authority for who owns a lobby:** when the owner goes, by leaving or by not asking, the member who has been there longest owns it (a `Map` keeps the order they joined in). A lobby nobody is in is gone. A kicked player is out of that lobby for as long as it lasts.
  - **Quick join is find-or-create in one request,** so two players asking at once can't both make a lobby the other missed: the fullest public, open lobby of the game with `seats` free whose data matches `filter`, or a new one as `create` says. `seats` is how a party asks for room for all of it. That's matchmaking's first form; a queue with rules of its own would answer the same question behind another request.
  - **Limits** (`LIMITS` in `lobbies.mjs`): 16 KiB a request, 20,000 lobbies, 4 lobbies a player (a party and the match it's going to), 256 members, 16 keys of 32 characters with values of 256, 50 lobbies a list, and 20 requests a second an address with 100 at once (`429` past that). The address is Fly's `Fly-Client-IP` behind its proxy, as for sockets.
  - **Statistics:** `/admin/stats` gained `lobbies` and `lobbyMembers`, and `totals` gained `lobbiesCreated`, `lobbyJoins`, `lobbyQuickJoins` and `lobbyMembersGone`. Counts only, as before.
  - It's in memory, on the one relay instance, like rooms: a restart ends every lobby, and its members' next request is `404`.
  - Tests: `relay/lobbies.test.mjs`, 12 tests over real HTTP on this machine with the clock `relay.test.mjs` has, so held answers and absence are checked to the millisecond with no waiting: making, joining and leaving with the owner passing on; full, closed and unknown lobbies; lists by data and seats; quick join filling the fullest and making one, with a party's seats; the owner's changes and a member's own; kicking, also while the kicked waits; held answers; absence; each refusal; other sites' pages; the rate limit; statistics. The `relay` test in CMake runs both files, and `AGENTS.md` names both for a relay-only change. 34 of 34 passed on Windows with Node 24.15.
  - Not built: tickets and a queue; Steam or any verified identity; chat (a match has its own); a lobby surviving a relay restart; a socket in place of held requests.

### Relay statistics

- The website can read aggregate relay statistics with `GET /admin/stats` on the relay's existing HTTP port. `TIDE_RELAY_STATS_TOKEN` is a secret shared with the website; it supplies `Authorization: Bearer <token>`. `createRelay` also takes `statsToken` for tests. Without a configured token every request is refused, as are missing or wrong tokens (`401`); authenticated methods other than GET get `405`, and this route refuses WebSocket upgrades (`400`). Token comparison uses `timingSafeEqual`, and every response from this route says `Cache-Control: no-store`. The intended production caller uses Fly's private network, `http://purrengine-relay.internal:8080`, with both apps in the same organization. No room codes, match keys, addresses or individual peer identifiers are returned.
- A snapshot has `instance` (a UUID for this relay instance), `uptimeSeconds` (from the relay's own monotonic clock), `rooms`, `connections`, `pendingJoins`, and `totals`. `rooms` counts registered room advertisements, not confirmed running matches: a host closing its socket removes the room immediately, but a dead connection can remain until the pings find it (up to about 50 seconds with the current intervals). Recently ended matches are held separately and do not count. `connections` counts accepted signaling WebSockets, including hosts, negotiating joiners, migration claimants and connections not yet assigned to a room. `pendingJoins` counts room joiners plus queued migration claimants.
- Totals are counters since this instance started: `roomsCreated` counts every registration, including reconnects and migration; `joinsAccepted` counts accepted introductions, including migration joins; `joinsMissing` and `joinsFull` count those join responses; `migrationsRequested` counts valid incoming migration requests, once each regardless of internal retries; `migrationsRejected` counts `taken` and `ended` migration responses. These are attempts and registrations, not unique matches or people, and an introduction does not say its WebRTC connection succeeded. The UUID changes when the relay restarts, so a caller does not subtract the new counters from the previous instance's.
- Actual concurrent players are not measured: joined players close their signaling socket after the host's first data-channel packet (`backend_receive` in `platform/src/rooms.c`, `channel.onmessage` in `platform/web/tide.js`). Adding player counts would need an agreed report from the host or players, which is not built. Sample history alone can miss activity between snapshots or immediately before a crash or autostop; the relay has no durable analytics log. Its Fly machine still stops when idle, and private `.internal` requests do not start stopped machines. A missing sample means idle or unreachable, not a measured zero.
- `relay/relay.test.mjs` checks auth failure and disabled credentials, allowed methods and aggregate schema, room/connection/join counters through closes, ended rooms and migration outcomes, and pending migration claims without double-counting internal retries. These use the existing fake clock. All 22 relay tests passed on Windows with Node 24.19; the relay-only change needs no runtime or browser test.

### Desktop WebRTC

- Desktop WebRTC is our own (`platform/src/rtc/`): P-256, ChaCha20-Poly1305 and the hashes, ICE with STUN and TURN, DTLS 1.2 and SCTP, only as much of each as a data channel needs, written from the RFCs with no third-party code, so games carry no license terms for it. Browsers are its check: it has to work against Chrome's and Firefox's (`platform/tests/rooms.mjs`). Desktop games reach the relay at `wss://` with the system's TLS (`src/rtc/tls.c`): SChannel on Windows (`secur32`), Secure Transport on macOS (the Security framework), and OpenSSL's libssl on Linux, loaded at run time rather than linked, so nothing ships with games; without it, Linux games can't reach the relay. Android apps can load no OpenSSL, so there it's Java's `SSLEngine`, through JNI. Browsers hide their local address behind `.local` names, which desktop games don't look up (hearing mDNS answers means listening on port 5353, which Windows' firewall asks players about): browsers' checks still reach the desktop side, which learns their address from them.
  - A peer is connecting until its channel is open, and connecting has an end, one way or the other (`rtc_peer.state`): ICE has 30 seconds to find a pair, DTLS 30 for its handshake, and SCTP 20 from when it starts for its association and the channel on it, both (`CONNECT_TIMEOUT` in `sctp.c`, until `rtc_sctp.connected`). Until 2026-10-06 SCTP's time was the association's alone. Ours is up from their COOKIE ECHO on, whether our COOKIE ACK reaches them or not, and from there the side that opened the channel waited for its acknowledgement, and the other for the open, with no end, while ICE's consent and DTLS held: the peer was never open and never failed, so the room said nothing (`pump` in `platform/src/rooms.c` fails a joiner's room when its peer does), and a host kept such a joiner's peer for as long as its consent held. What ended a joining player's wait was only its session's own 15 seconds for a room's host to first answer (`ROOM_TIMEOUT` in `engine/src/session.c`), as a time-out, where there's a session: the rooms test's echo cases have none, and waited their whole 45 seconds. A browser whose association never came up is no help: Chrome goes on echoing its cookie after 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 seconds, and sends no ABORT within 45. It's the deadline there was, over all it was for: nothing is sent again for it, and nothing waits longer.
  - An answer to an ICE check counts for its pair only from the address the check went to, the same way (straight, or through TURN), as RFC 8445 has it (7.2.5.2.1; `on_response` in `ice.c`, which is handed where the answer came from). From anywhere else the pair has failed, and on the pair in use it's no consent. A socket on every address answers by whichever of them the way back leaves by: on Linux and macOS, a check from a loopback socket to this machine's network address arrives, and is answered from 127.0.0.1. Taken by its transaction alone, as it was, that made the pair to the network address the one in use: everything was sent there from a loopback socket, what came back arrived on another pair, and consent was kept by the checks' answers alone. What they send comes from where they answer from, and their own check from there is how that pair is found (peer-reflexive): the pair Windows always ended on, where such a check never arrives. Seen on Linux with two desktop players, the host's socket on every address and the joiner's on loopback (`TIDE_RTC_LOCAL`), before and after.
  - SCTP starts no later than DTLS's first data for it (`dtls_in` and `start_sctp` in `peer.c`). A DTLS server is open as it answers their last flight, in the middle of reading what came, and their INIT can be the next thing it reads, in that update or that datagram. SCTP used to start once the reading was done, so that INIT was dropped, and the association came up a second later from both sides' INITs crossing: it works, at the cost of that second, on a path nothing else takes.
  - DTLS is given their datagrams once it can answer them, and not before (`dtls_answers`, `hold` and `give_held` in `peer.c`): once it's started, which for a server is at their description, and ICE has a pair in use. It answers what it reads as it reads it, and what it sends with no pair in use goes nowhere (`rtc_ice_send`). So a ClientHello read too early had its answer lost, and the handshake waited for a flight to be sent again: half a second, with our stack on both sides. Their ClientHello does come that early. They send it as soon as their own ICE has a pair, ICE hands over what comes from any address it knows, in use or not (`on_datagram` in `ice.c`), and the answer to our own check can be behind it: late, lost, or from another address than the check went to (above). Chrome, hosting, sends it earlier still, once a check of its own was answered, which takes our description alone: before its answer reaches us, when that's slower through the relay than a check is between the two machines, and nothing here says yet which side is the server (seen with its signals held back 150 ms by hand; Firefox waits until we picked a pair). What comes before DTLS can answer is held, in the order it came (`rtc_peer.held`), and DTLS gets it the moment it can: in the update that reads the answer to our check, before anything that came after. What code relies on:
    - It holds 4 datagrams (`RTC_PEER_HELD`): a ClientHello can take more than one, and they send it again meanwhile (Chrome's is 1.4 KB in two datagrams, sent again 60 ms later). One that finds no room is lost as one on its way would be, and theirs comes again.
    - Only while this side is, or may still be, the server: a client is sent nothing before its ClientHello, and what was held is dropped when their description says we're the client.
    - Nothing is timed, nothing is sent again for it, and no deadline moved: a server's 30 seconds for its handshake still start at their description.
  - A DTLS client starts, which sends its ClientHello, in the update that finds ICE's pair (`rtc_peer_update` in `peer.c`): after that update read what came, as ICE finds its pair in what's read and nowhere else (`select_pair` in `ice.c`, from the answer to our check or from their check that picks the pair). Until 2026-10-06 the start was looked at before the reading, so the ClientHello went in the next update, a frame of the game later. With our stack a room's host is the client (a peer that didn't offer answers `active`). What that update read after the pair was found is no answer to a ClientHello that hadn't gone yet, and is dropped as it was (`hold`). On Linux with two desktop players whose frames are 2 ms apart (`rooms_native.c`), from the host's pair to the joiner reading its ClientHello: a median of 3 to 4 ms before and 1 after (20 runs each, twice).
  - A check of theirs that came before their description is checked back the moment their password is there (`owed`, `check_back` and `rtc_ice_set_remote` in `ice.c`, which takes the time for it), as RFC 8445 has a triggered check done once the credentials are known (7.3, 7.3.1.4). Such a check is answered at once, which only takes our own password, and makes a pair of the address it came from; the check back is signed with theirs, so it's owed until then. It used to wait its turn after that as well: the pair ranks below the addresses their description names (a peer-reflexive candidate's priority against a host's), each of which was checked first, 20 ms apart (`CHECK_EVERY`), while they could already send on the pair their check found.
  - Those two were found together (2026-10-06), on Linux with two desktop players, the host's socket on every address and the joiner's on loopback (`TIDE_RTC_LOCAL`): the host's check arrives before its answer does, the joiner's check to the address the answer names is answered from 127.0.0.1 and counts for nothing (above), the pair their check found waits its 20 ms, and the host's ClientHello arrives in them. From their answer to the channel open, over 10 runs each: a median of 549 ms before (9 of the 10 lost the half second), 63 with the hold alone (the ClientHello held for those 20 ms), and 61 with both, the pair in use 2 ms after their answer where it took 23.
